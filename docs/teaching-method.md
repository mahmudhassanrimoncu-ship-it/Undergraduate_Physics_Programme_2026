# Teaching Method: Managing Cognitive Load

*How every lecture, recitation, problem set and tutorial in the programme is designed. Adopted October 2026 at Rimon's request, from Scott H. Young, "Cognitive Load Theory and its Applications for Learning" (scotthyoung.com), which summarises John Sweller's cognitive load theory.*

Where this document and a course syllabus disagree about *how* something is taught, this document wins. It changes no grading weights: those stay as in [assessment-and-grading.md](assessment-and-grading.md).

---

## 1. The theory in five sentences

1. **Working memory is small for new material and effectively unlimited for mastered material.** Juggling more than a few new pieces at once makes them fall out, and nothing is learnt.
2. **Intrinsic load** is the effort needed to learn the pattern itself. **Extraneous load** is effort wasted on anything else: distractions, badly laid-out materials, or having to guess a method that could have been taught. **Germane load** is extra effort that deepens learning (self-explanation, retrieval practice, varied practice), but only helps when there is spare capacity.
3. **Physics and mathematics have high element interactivity.** Many pieces must be held at once (a free-body diagram, Newton's law, the constraint, the calculus), so they hit the limit sooner than subjects that are merely large.
4. **Novices learn faster from worked examples than from solving problems**, because problem solving spends working memory on searching instead of on the method. As expertise grows this **reverses**: then solving from memory beats reading examples.
5. **"Problem solving" is not a general skill.** It comes from many stored patterns plus fluent, automatic component skills, so the fastest route is the right method first, then practice.

## 2. The rules this programme follows

| Effect (Young / Sweller) | Rule in this programme |
|---|---|
| Worked-example effect | Every new method is shown first as a fully worked example, before you are asked to use it. |
| Practice after examples | Practice moves from *examples* → *completion problems* (part-solved) → *full problems* → *varied problems*. |
| Expertise reversal | Support is removed topic by topic as you show you have the pattern. Once a method is mastered, you solve first and see no example. |
| Goal-free effect | New topics can open with a "find everything you can" warm-up instead of a single target. Used only where the possible moves are few, as in kinematics, circuits or geometry. |
| Split-attention effect | Notes are integrated: labels sit on the diagram, each equation sits next to the figure it describes, and you never flip pages to follow one argument. |
| Extraneous load | One primary text per course, one weekly release with everything in one place, consistent notation, and no hunting for the method. |
| Germane load: self-explanation | After feedback, you explain the solutions to the problems you found hard. This is now part of the tutorial. |
| Germane load: retrieval practice | Weekly quizzes, Collections, and a short prerequisite warm-up at the start of each lecture. |
| Variable practice helps only below the limit | Mixed and interleaved problems come late in a topic and in revision, never on the first day of a new idea. |
| Element interactivity → build component fluency | If a topic overloads you, we drop back and drill the components it depends on (algebra, vectors, derivatives) until they are automatic. |
| Slow down early (Feynman technique) | Confusion is found and fixed in the same week. A missed early idea makes later lectures wasted time. |

## 3. Lectures

A live lecture ("PHY 101 L5") runs in this order:

1. **Prerequisite warm-up (5 min).** Two or three quick retrieval questions on the pieces today's topic depends on. If they are shaky, I fix them before going on, because a missing component is the commonest cause of overload.
2. **Learning objectives.** What you will be able to do afterwards.
3. **Chunks of one new idea each.** For each chunk:
   - the idea and its derivation, in small steps with no skipped assumptions;
   - **a fully worked example straight away**, with a one-line reason for each step;
   - a **concept check** you answer before reading on.
4. **A paired problem.** A problem that mirrors the last worked example, with the example still visible.
5. **Summary**, plus the matching textbook pages and the MIT OCW lecture as a second source.
6. **Load check.** You rate the mental effort of the lecture from 1 (very low) to 9 (very high), and name the step where you got lost, if any (see §7).

Notes are written to avoid split attention. Each figure carries its own labels, and every equation is restated where it is used rather than referred back to by number.

## 4. Recitations

Recitations follow a **fading ladder** on the week's method:

1. **Quiz** (10 min): retrieval practice on last week's material.
2. **Worked example**, studied together. You explain each step back in your own words.
3. **Completion problem.** The same type of problem with the later steps left blank for you.
4. **Full problem.** You solve it; I give hints only when you are stuck.
5. **Goal-free variant** (when it fits): "here is the set-up; find every quantity you can."

If you are already fluent (the quiz is strong and the effort rating is low), we skip straight to step 4. Making an expert re-read examples is wasted load.

## 5. Problem sets

Each weekly set has up to three parts:

| Part | What it is | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Example pairs** | Each problem comes with a solved twin. Study the twin, then solve the problem without looking back. | First 1–2 weeks of a new topic |
| **B. Core problems** | Standard problems on the week's material, no examples given. | Every week, the bulk of the set |
| **C. Mixed / stretch** | Interleaved problems from earlier weeks, plus one harder or less familiar problem. | Once a topic is secure, and in revision |

The share of Part A shrinks as each course goes on: it is largest in Year 1 and small by Years 3–4 (expertise reversal). It is fixed per topic, though, not per year. A brand-new subject in Year 3, such as general relativity, starts again with more examples.

**Honour code note.** The solved twins in Part A are part of the set, so using them is allowed. The rule against looking up the solutions *to the assigned problems* before you submit still stands ([Handbook §9](handbook.md#9-honour-code)).

**After grading.** Full solutions are released after the deadline. Before the tutorial, read the solution to every problem you lost marks on, and be ready to explain it.

## 6. Tutorials

Tutorials keep their Oxford form and their 0–3 score, with one change. Every tutorial now includes a **self-explanation round**. You talk through the posted solution to the problem you found hardest: why each step is taken, and what would change if the set-up changed. This turns feedback into germane load instead of a mark you glance at.

## 7. Measuring load and adapting

After each lecture and each problem set you give one number, your **mental effort from 1 to 9**, plus where you got lost, if anywhere. This rating is the standard Paas scale from the cognitive load research literature (an addition, not something Young's essay prescribes). Together with quiz and problem-set scores, it decides the next step:

| Effort | Performance | Reading | What I do next |
|---|---|---|---|
| High (7–9) | Low | Overloaded | Slow down. More worked examples and smaller chunks. Drill the prerequisite components. Re-teach the step where you got lost before moving on |
| High (7–9) | Good | At the limit, coping | Keep the pace, but no stretch problems yet. Add retrieval practice |
| Medium (4–6) | Good | The productive zone | Carry on. Add varied and interleaved practice (germane load) |
| Low (1–3) | Good | Pattern mastered | Fade the support: fewer examples, problem-first recitations, more Part C |
| Low (1–3) | Low | Not engaged, or a gap you haven't noticed | A diagnostic question set to find the gap |

The ratings are kept per course in the gradebook notes and summarised in the Semester Report. They are never part of the grade.

## 8. Prerequisite fluency

Because physics is high in element interactivity, weak components cost the most. Three things guard against this:

- The **Orientation Term** diagnostics and bridging lectures (Nov–Dec 2026) find gaps before Semester 1.
- Each lecture's **warm-up** and each week's **quiz** keep earlier components fresh.
- When a gap shows up, you get a short **fluency drill**: many quick, easy problems on the single component until it is automatic. Time spent there is repaid in every later topic.

## 9. Your side of the bargain

Cognitive load is also shaped by how you study:

1. **Study the examples before the problems.** Read the worked examples in the lecture notes and the textbook before you start the set.
2. **Say "I'm lost" early.** Do it in the lecture or the same evening. One unresolved early idea can make the rest of the course harder than it needs to be.
3. **Explain it to yourself.** When a step doesn't make sense, write out the explanation in plain words (the Feynman technique). The place where you can't is the gap.
4. **Remove extraneous load while studying.** No background video or phone. Keep the notes and the problem side by side.
5. **Give the effort rating honestly.** It is the instrument that keeps the load at the right level.
